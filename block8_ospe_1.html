01

Pathology

4×OSPE + 2×OSVE = 60 marks

Neoplasia (Module 16) dominates this subject — general oncologic concepts plus gross/microscopic identification of benign vs malignant tumours — with systemic-pathology carry-overs from MSK, infectious disease and clinical pathology practicals.

N-Pa-008

Station 1 · Gross & Microscopic ID — Benign vs Malignant Tumours

  • Lipoma — encapsulated, lobulated mass of mature adipocytes indistinguishable from normal fat; the softest, most mobile "swelling" on gross exam.
  • Leiomyoma — well-circumscribed, whorled bundles of interlacing smooth muscle fascicles ("whorled/onion-skin" appearance on cut section).
  • Fibroadenoma of breast — biphasic tumour (stroma + epithelium), pericanalicular (stroma surrounds round ducts) or intracanalicular (stroma compresses ducts into slits) growth pattern, "mouse-eared", freely mobile "breast mouse" on exam.
  • DCIS & Bowen's disease — malignant cells confined within duct/epidermis, basement membrane intact, no stromal invasion; Bowen's disease is essentially SCC-in-situ of the skin.
  • Adenocarcinoma (colon) — gland-forming malignant epithelium, "back-to-back" glands with scant intervening stroma and a desmoplastic (fibrous) reaction.
  • Squamous cell carcinoma — keratin pearls + intercellular bridges; common sites: skin, lip, oesophagus, cervix, larynx, lung.

Growth rateSlowRapid
BorderEncapsulated / circumscribedInfiltrative, irregular

QHow is DCIS different from invasive ductal carcinoma?▸

DCIS is confined by an intact basement membrane with no stromal invasion — it cannot metastasise until it progresses to invasive carcinoma.

QName the malignant tumour of vascular endothelium.▸

Angiosarcoma.

QWhat does the "T-shaped" abnormal cell on the anaplasia slide represent?▸

An abnormal (tripolar/multipolar) mitotic figure — a hallmark of anaplasia reflecting loss of cell-cycle control.

QHow would you tell a fibroadenoma from a phyllodes tumour on gross exam?▸

Fibroadenoma is small, firm, sharply circumscribed and slow-growing; a phyllodes tumour is typically larger, may show leaf-like clefts on cut section, and can recur/behave more aggressively.

N-Pa-002/003/007

Station 2 · Neoplasia — Concepts, TP53, Grading & Staging

Neoplasia (Willis) — an abnormal mass of tissue, growth of which exceeds and is uncoordinated with normal tissue, and persists after cessation of the stimuli that evoked the change.

  • TP53 — "guardian of the genome"; arrests the cell cycle at G1/S for DNA repair or triggers apoptosis; mutated in >50% of human cancers; germline mutation → Li-Fraumeni syndrome.
  • Grading — degree of differentiation (how closely tumour cells resemble the tissue of origin).
  • Staging (TNM) — anatomical extent of spread; Tumour size/extent, Nodal involvement, Metastasis. Carries greater prognostic weight than grade.
  • Dysplasia — disordered, reversible pre-neoplastic epithelial change.
  • Invasion vs metastasis — invasion = local spread through the basement membrane; metastasis = spread to a distant site via lymphatics, blood, or seeding of body cavities.
  • Carcinogenic agents — chemical (aflatoxin → HCC, aromatic amines → bladder cancer, benzo[a]pyrene in smoke), physical (UV, ionizing radiation), biological (HPV → cervical cancer, HBV/HCV → HCC, H. pylori → gastric MALT lymphoma/adenocarcinoma).
  • Tumour markers — AFP (hepatocellular carcinoma, yolk sac tumour), CEA (colorectal), CA-125 (ovarian), CA 19-9 (pancreatic), PSA (prostate), hCG (choriocarcinoma/germ cell tumours) — used for monitoring/response to treatment more than primary diagnosis.
  • Paraneoplastic syndrome — symptom complex in a cancer patient not directly caused by tumour bulk/invasion/metastasis, but by secreted substances (e.g. PTHrP → hypercalcaemia in squamous lung cancer; ACTH → Cushing's in small-cell lung cancer).
QWhich carries more prognostic value — grading or staging?▸

Staging — the anatomical extent of spread predicts outcome more strongly than the degree of differentiation.

QName one hallmark of cancer related to blood supply.▸

Sustained angiogenesis, driven mainly by VEGF secretion from tumour cells.

QWhich tumour marker is raised in hepatocellular carcinoma?▸

Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP).

QWhat germline syndrome results from inherited TP53 mutation?▸

Li-Fraumeni syndrome — multiple early-onset cancers (sarcoma, breast, brain, adrenal).

MS2-Orth010 / MS2-Rh001

Station 4 · Septic Arthritis & Rheumatoid Arthritis

  • Septic arthritis — acute purulent joint infection, commonly S. aureus (gonococcus in young sexually active adults). Confirmed by joint aspirate: turbid fluid, WBC >50,000/µL (mostly neutrophils), positive Gram stain/culture.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis — chronic autoimmune, symmetric small-joint polyarthritis. Pathology: synovial hyperplasia (pannus) erodes cartilage and bone. Serology: RF (IgM anti-Fc of IgG) + anti-CCP (more specific).

QHow is septic arthritis confirmed in the lab?▸

Synovial fluid aspiration — Gram stain and culture, with WBC >50,000/µL, PMN predominant.

QWhat is pannus?▸

Inflamed, hyperplastic synovium with granulation tissue that erodes articular cartilage and subchondral bone in RA.

Scenario Station

Station 5 · Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy, TB & Urinalysis

  • DMD — X-linked recessive dystrophin-gene mutation. Child unable to climb stairs, uses hands to "walk up" own body from squatting (Gower's sign), calf pseudohypertrophy (fat/fibrous replacement of degenerated muscle), markedly raised CK.
  • Tuberculosis — caseating granuloma: epithelioid histiocytes + Langhans giant cells around central caseous necrosis; confirmed by Ziehl-Neelsen acid-fast stain.
  • Urinalysis — physical (colour, clarity), chemical/dipstick (protein, glucose, blood, leukocyte esterase, nitrites), microscopic (cells, casts, crystals). Culture medium: CLED agar (prevents Proteus swarming). Pyuria causes: UTI, renal TB, interstitial nephritis, appendicitis. Haematuria causes: stones, glomerulonephritis, malignancy, trauma, infection. Combined pyuria+haematuria: renal TB, infected stones, schistosomiasis, malignancy with secondary infection.
QWhat causes calf pseudohypertrophy in DMD?▸

Degenerated muscle fibres are replaced by fat and fibrous connective tissue, enlarging the calf despite weakness.

QWhat is the hallmark microscopic feature of a TB granuloma?▸

Central caseous (cheese-like) necrosis surrounded by epithelioid cells and Langhans-type multinucleated giant cells.

QWhich culture medium is used for urine, and why?▸

CLED agar — its electrolyte-deficient composition prevents swarming of Proteus while supporting most urinary pathogens.

MS2-Pa-002 (contd.)

Station 6 · Bone & Soft-Tissue Lesions — Osteomyelitis, Osteosarcoma & Hemangioma

  • Acute osteomyelitis — neutrophilic infiltrate in bone/marrow, usually haematogenous spread of S. aureus in children (metaphysis of long bones).
  • Chronic osteomyelitis — sequestrum (necrotic dead bone fragment) enclosed by an involucrum (surrounding shell of new reactive periosteal bone); may discharge via a sinus tract.
  • Osteosarcoma (osteogenic sarcoma) — malignant osteoid-producing tumour, typically in the metaphysis of long bones around the knee in adolescents; X-ray shows a "sunburst" periosteal reaction and a Codman triangle where periosteum is lifted at the tumour margin.
  • Hemangioma — benign vascular tumour (capillary or cavernous type); histology shows blood-filled vascular channels lined by bland endothelium.
QDifferentiate sequestrum from involucrum.▸

Sequestrum is the necrotic dead bone fragment; involucrum is the surrounding shell of new, reactive periosteal bone that forms around it.

QWhat is a Codman triangle, and what does it indicate?▸

A triangular area of elevated periosteum at the margin of an aggressive bone lesion (classically osteosarcoma) — a radiological sign of rapid, aggressive growth.

Microbiology & Parasitology

examined within Pathology's 60

Infectious Diseases (Module 17) territory — organism identification, culture media, serology, and parasitology.

ID-Pa-009

Station 1 · Gram-Positive Cocci — Identification & Differentiation

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae — lancet-shaped gram-positive diplococcus, alpha-haemolytic, optochin-sensitive, bile-soluble.
  • Staphylococcus aureus — gram-positive cocci in clusters, coagulase-positive, golden colonies, beta-haemolytic.
  • Streptococcus classification — Lancefield groups (A = S. pyogenes, B = S. agalactiae) combined with haemolysis pattern (alpha = partial/green, beta = complete/clear, gamma = none).
  • Group B strep confirmation — positive CAMP test (arrowhead-shaped enhanced haemolysis near a Staph streak) + positive hippurate hydrolysis.
QWhat does optochin sensitivity distinguish?▸

Optochin-sensitive = S. pneumoniae; optochin-resistant = viridans streptococci.

QWhat does a positive coagulase test indicate?▸

Staphylococcus aureus, differentiating it from coagulase-negative staphylococci (S. epidermidis, S. saprophyticus).

ID-Pa-001 / ID-Pa-011

Station 2 · Gram-Negative Organisms & Culture Media

  • Vibrio cholerae — comma-shaped; grows as yellow colonies on TCBS agar (sucrose fermenter).
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa — blue-green pigment (pyocyanin), grape-like odour, oxidase-positive.
  • Shigella → bacillary dysentery. Yersinia pestis → plague ("Black Death").
  • Chocolate agar organisms — Haemophilus influenzae and Neisseria spp. (fastidious; need factors V & X released by heat-lysed RBCs).
  • Corynebacterium diphtheriae — club-shaped, shows metachromatic (Babes-Ernst) granules on Albert's/Loeffler's stain.

QWhich agar is used for cholera, and what colour do colonies appear?▸

TCBS agar — Vibrio cholerae ferments sucrose, producing yellow colonies.

QWhy does H. influenzae require chocolate agar rather than plain blood agar?▸

Heating lyses the RBCs, releasing factor V (NAD) and factor X (haemin), both required for growth — these remain unavailable in unheated blood agar.

QWhat does a positive urease test suggest?▸

An organism such as Proteus, which hydrolyses urea to ammonia — relevant in UTI, where it alkalinises urine and predisposes to struvite stones.

ID-Pa-010

Station 3 · Culture Sensitivity Reports & Antibiogram Interpretation

A disc-diffusion antibiogram reports each antibiotic as Sensitive (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R), based on the diameter of the clear zone of inhibition around each antibiotic disc compared to standard breakpoints.

  • A wider zone of inhibition generally means the organism is more susceptible to that antibiotic.
  • The report guides de-escalation from broad-spectrum empirical therapy to a narrow-spectrum, sensitivity-guided antibiotic.
  • MRSA screening uses a cefoxitin disc as a surrogate marker for methicillin resistance.
QWhat does a "resistant" report mean for a given antibiotic?▸

The organism is able to grow despite clinically achievable concentrations of that drug — it should not be used to treat this infection.

ID-Pa-002 / ID-Pa-012

Station 4 · Parasitology — Protozoa, Helminths & Cestodes

  • Entamoeba histolytica — trophozoite (may contain ingested RBCs, diagnostic of invasive amoebiasis) vs quadrinucleate cyst (infective stage).
  • Giardia lamblia — pear-shaped trophozoite with two nuclei ("old-man-face" appearance); oval cyst with up to 4 nuclei.
  • Ascaris lumbricoides — faeco-oral transmission, adults reside in small intestine; thick mammillated (bumpy) egg shell; larval migration causes Loeffler's pneumonitis; heavy loads cause intestinal obstruction.
  • Trichuris trichiura — barrel-shaped egg with bipolar plugs ("whipworm").
  • Schistosoma egg — identified by characteristic lateral or terminal spine (species-dependent).
  • Taenia solium / saginata — eggs are identical (striated shell with hexacanth embryo); species distinguished by scolex (hooks in T. solium) and proglottid morphology.
  • Echinococcus granulosus — hydatid cyst with brood capsules and protoscolices bearing hooklets.
  • Hymenolepis nana — oval egg with polar filaments between the embryo and outer shell.
  • Plasmodium — intra-RBC ring forms on smear; causes anaemia via haemolysis.
  • Tinea capitis — caused by Microsporum and Trichophyton species.
QWhich egg has bipolar plugs?▸

Trichuris trichiura — barrel-shaped with a translucent plug at each pole.

QHow do you distinguish Taenia solium from Taenia saginata eggs on stool microscopy?▸

You can't — the eggs are morphologically identical; species is identified by the scolex (hooks present in T. solium, absent in T. saginata) or gravid proglottid uterine branching.

QWhat complication can heavy Ascaris infestation in a child cause?▸

Intestinal obstruction from a bolus of worms; also biliary/pancreatic duct obstruction if worms migrate.

ID-Pa-007

Station 5 · Sexually Transmitted Infections — Chlamydia & Syphilis

  • Chlamydia — obligate intracellular; biphasic life cycle: infectious extracellular elementary body → replicating intracellular reticulate body.
  • Syphilis (Treponema pallidum) — primary (painless chancre) → secondary (rash, condyloma lata) → tertiary (gumma). Screening: VDRL/RPR (non-treponemal, cheap, false positives possible). Confirmatory: TPHA/TPPA/FTA-ABS (treponemal, specific).
  • UTI — second most common cause: Staphylococcus saprophyticus (young, sexually active women).
QWhich chlamydial form is infectious?▸

The elementary body — metabolically inactive but capable of infecting new host cells.

QDistinguish VDRL from TPHA.▸

VDRL = non-treponemal screening test (can give false positives in autoimmune disease, pregnancy). TPHA = treponemal confirmatory test, specific for T. pallidum.

ID-Pa-009 / ID-Pa-015

Station 6 · Staining & Rapid Identification Tests

Gram stain steps: smear → heat fix → crystal violet (1 min) → iodine mordant (1 min) → decolourise with 95% alcohol → counterstain with safranin → examine.

Ziehl-Neelsen (AFB) stain: carbol fuchsin with heat → decolourise with acid-alcohol → counterstain with methylene blue; acid-fast bacilli (Mycobacterium) retain the pink/red stain due to their mycolic-acid-rich cell wall.

TestDifferentiates
CatalaseStaphylococcus (+, bubbles) vs Streptococcus (–)
CoagulaseS. aureus (+) vs coagulase-negative staph (–)
OxidasePseudomonas/Neisseria/Vibrio (+) vs Enterobacteriaceae (–)
QWhat does the catalase test detect?▸

Presence of catalase enzyme, which breaks down H₂O₂ into water and oxygen (bubbles) — positive in Staphylococcus, negative in Streptococcus.

QWhy do acid-fast bacilli resist decolourisation in ZN stain?▸

Their cell wall is rich in mycolic acid (a waxy lipid), which binds carbol fuchsin tightly and resists the acid-alcohol decolouriser.

ID-Ph-009

Station 1 · Dispensing & Prescription Writing

A prescription is a physician's legal written order to the pharmacist — written legibly, in ink.

PartMeaning
SuperscriptionThe Rx symbol
InscriptionName & strength of the drug(s)
SubscriptionDirections to the pharmacist (how to prepare/dispense)
Signatura (Transcription)Directions to the patient — "sig."
  • APC Powder — a classical antipyretic-analgesic combination of Aspirin, Phenacetin and Caffeine; phenacetin is largely avoided today (nephrotoxicity/haemolysis risk) and often substituted with paracetamol in modern practice.
  • Carminative mixture — relieves flatulence and colicky abdominal pain; typically contains an antacid (e.g. sodium bicarbonate) plus aromatic oils (dill, ginger, peppermint) that relax GI smooth muscle and expel trapped gas.
  • Conditions to practise prescription writing for: UTI (nitrofurantoin/fosfomycin), acute bacillary dysentery (ciprofloxacin/azithromycin), malaria (chloroquine or ACT depending on species/resistance), amoebiasis (metronidazole + a luminal agent e.g. diloxanide furoate), helminthic infections (albendazole/mebendazole), hepatitis B & C (tenofovir/entecavir for B; direct-acting antivirals e.g. sofosbuvir for C), dengue (supportive care with paracetamol — avoid NSAIDs/aspirin due to bleeding risk), COVID-19 (supportive ± antivirals per severity), typhoid fever (ceftriaxone/azithromycin), community-acquired pneumonia (amoxicillin/azithromycin), and pulmonary TB (ATT — the RIPE regimen).
QWhat is the Subscription part of a prescription?▸

The pharmacist-directed instructions on how to compound/prepare and dispense the drug.

QWhy should NSAIDs/aspirin be avoided in dengue fever?▸

They increase bleeding risk in a disease already prone to thrombocytopenia and haemorrhage — paracetamol is preferred for fever/pain control.

QWhy has phenacetin fallen out of use in APC powder?▸

Long-term use is linked to analgesic nephropathy and haemolytic anaemia; paracetamol is used as a safer substitute.

ID-Ph-001/003/007

Station 2 · Antibiotics & Antifungals

  • Penicillin — inhibits transpeptidase (PBP), blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking (cell-wall synthesis inhibitor), bactericidal. Classes: natural (Pen G/V), antistaphylococcal (oxacillin), aminopenicillins (amoxicillin), antipseudomonal (piperacillin). β-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam) protect penicillins from bacterial β-lactamases.
  • Cephalosporins — 1st–5th generation; gram-negative coverage broadens with each generation; 5th generation (ceftaroline) covers MRSA.
  • Erythromycin (macrolide) — atypical pneumonia, penicillin-allergic patients, chlamydia, pertussis.
  • Rifampicin — 1st-line TB & leprosy, meningococcal/H. influenzae prophylaxis; induces cytochrome P450; turns body fluids red-orange (harmless).
  • Amphotericin B — polyene antifungal, binds ergosterol forming membrane pores; used for systemic mycoses. AE: nephrotoxicity, infusion-related "shake and bake" fever/chills, hypokalaemia.
  • Antituberculosis therapy (RIPE) — Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol for 2 months intensive phase, then Rifampicin + Isoniazid for 4 months continuation phase (standard WHO-recommended regimen for new pulmonary TB).
QWhy does rifampicin turn urine/tears red-orange, and is it dangerous?▸

It is an expected, harmless pigment effect of the drug itself — patients should simply be reassured.

QHow do β-lactamase inhibitors work?▸

They irreversibly bind and inactivate bacterial β-lactamase enzymes, protecting the co-administered penicillin from hydrolysis.

QWhich anti-TB drug is most associated with optic neuritis?▸

Ethambutol — monitor visual acuity and red-green colour discrimination.

N-Ph-002

Station 3 · Anticancer Pharmacology & Biologics

  • Log-kill hypothesis — each chemotherapy cycle kills a constant fraction (not fixed number) of tumour cells, hence the need for repeated cycles to eradicate disease.
  • Resistance mechanisms — increased drug efflux (P-glycoprotein/MDR1), decreased uptake/activation, increased DNA repair, altered target enzyme, apoptosis evasion.
  • Methotrexate — folate antagonist; inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) → ↓ tetrahydrofolate → ↓ thymidylate & purine synthesis → blocks S-phase (cell-cycle specific). Rescue agent: leucovorin (folinic acid).
  • TNF-α inhibitors — infliximab (chimeric mAb), adalimumab (fully human mAb), etanercept (TNF receptor–Fc fusion protein) — used in RA, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, IBD.
  • Glucocorticoids in cancer cocktails — used for their lympholytic effect (e.g. in CHOP regimen for lymphoma), for reducing peritumoural oedema (brain metastases), and as antiemetics alongside chemotherapy.
QWhat rescues a patient from methotrexate toxicity?▸

Leucovorin (folinic acid) — bypasses DHFR blockade, replenishing tetrahydrofolate for normal cells.

QName three TNF-α inhibitors.▸

Infliximab, adalimumab, and etanercept.

Autonomic / NMJ + NSAIDs

Station 4 · Neuromuscular Blockers & NSAIDs

  • NM blockers — Depolarizing (succinylcholine) vs Non-depolarizing/competitive (tubocurarine, atracurium, vecuronium, rocuronium).
  • Succinylcholine — persistent depolarization of the motor end-plate → initial fasciculations then flaccid paralysis; ultra-short acting (hydrolysed by plasma pseudocholinesterase); used for rapid sequence intubation. AE: hyperkalaemia (dangerous in burns/trauma), malignant hyperthermia, prolonged apnoea in pseudocholinesterase deficiency.
  • Frog rectus abdominis / animal-model practicals — atracurium (or another skeletal muscle relaxant) is applied in increasing concentrations to a frog rectus abdominis muscle preparation; the resulting dose-dependent contraction blockade is plotted on a graph, and reversal is demonstrated with neostigmine (an anticholinesterase) — illustrating competitive antagonism at the neuromuscular junction.
  • NSAIDs — non-selective COX inhibitors (aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac) vs selective COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib).
  • Aspirin side effects — GI ulceration/bleeding, tinnitus (salicylism), Reye syndrome in children with viral illness, bleeding tendency (irreversible COX-1/platelet inhibition).
QWhy is succinylcholine dangerous in a burns patient?▸

Up-regulated extrajunctional ACh receptors cause massive potassium efflux on depolarization → life-threatening hyperkalaemia and cardiac arrest.

QHow does neostigmine reverse a non-depolarizing block on the frog rectus preparation?▸

By inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, it raises synaptic acetylcholine levels, which out-competes the non-depolarizing blocker at the receptor.

QWhy should aspirin be avoided in a febrile child with a viral infection?▸

Risk of Reye syndrome — acute hepatic failure with encephalopathy.

MS2-Ph-001/002/003

Station 5 · Prescription Writing — RA, Gout & OA + Miscellaneous Drugs

  • Rheumatoid arthritis — NSAID/corticosteroid for symptom control + a DMARD (methotrexate first-line) started early to slow joint destruction; biologics (TNF-α inhibitors) added if inadequate response.
  • Acute gout — NSAID (e.g. indomethacin) or colchicine (if NSAID contraindicated) or a short corticosteroid course; never start allopurinol during an acute attack (can prolong/worsen it).
  • Chronic gout — xanthine oxidase inhibitor (allopurinol) or a uricosuric agent, started once the acute attack has settled.
  • Osteoarthritis — paracetamol first-line, topical/oral NSAIDs for flares, plus non-pharmacological measures (weight loss, physiotherapy).
  • Bisphosphonates (e.g. alendronate) — inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption; used in osteoporosis, Paget's disease, hypercalcaemia of malignancy, bone metastases.
  • Ringer Lactate — contains Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻ and lactate (converted to bicarbonate by the liver); infused via standard IV giving sets (macro-drip 20 drops/mL, micro-drip 60 drops/mL).
  • Albendazole — broad-spectrum anthelmintic; inhibits parasite β-tubulin polymerization → ↓ glucose uptake; used for ascariasis, hookworm, hydatid disease, neurocysticercosis.
QWhy shouldn't allopurinol be started during an acute gout attack?▸

Sudden changes in serum urate can mobilise crystals and prolong/worsen the acute flare — it should be started only after the attack has settled.

QWhat is the mechanism of albendazole?▸

It inhibits microtubule (β-tubulin) polymerization in the parasite, impairing glucose uptake and killing the organism.

Forensic Medicine

1×OSPE (examined) = 08 marks

Only one 8-mark OSPE station is drawn from this subject in the actual exam — but the full practicals list is covered below so nothing on the syllabus is left unrevised.

Traumatology · Likely tested

Station 1 · Coup & Contrecoup Haemorrhage

  • Coup injury — brain injury directly beneath the point of impact; typical of a stationary head struck by a moving object.
  • Contrecoup injury — injury on the side opposite the impact; typical of a moving head striking a stationary object, due to the brain's inertial lag and cavitation on the far side.
QA moving head strikes a stationary wall — where is the brain injury expected?▸

Contrecoup — on the side opposite the point of contact.

Asphyxia · Likely tested

Station 2 · Asphyxial Deaths — Suffocation, Hanging, Choking

  • Smothering — external blockage of mouth/nose. Gagging — foreign material forced into the mouth/pharynx. Choking — internal airway obstruction by a foreign body.
  • Hanging — ligature + the body's own weight provides the constricting force; ligature mark is typically oblique, non-continuous, above the thyroid cartilage.
  • Strangulation — external force (not body weight) constricts the neck; mark is usually horizontal and continuous, at or below the thyroid cartilage.
QDifferentiate the ligature mark of hanging from strangulation.▸

Hanging: oblique, non-continuous, above the thyroid cartilage. Strangulation: horizontal, continuous, at/below the thyroid cartilage.

Drowning · Likely tested

Station 3 · Drowning — Mechanism & Froth

  • Mechanism — laryngospasm → hypoxia; wet drowning (water enters lungs) is far more common than dry drowning (~10–15%, persistent laryngospasm with little aspirated water).
  • Froth — fine, white, lathery froth at the mouth/nostrils, produced by air, water, mucus and surfactant churned by continued respiratory efforts — a classic external finding.
  • Freshwater vs saltwater — freshwater (hypotonic) causes haemodilution/haemolysis/hyperkalaemia; saltwater (hypertonic) causes pulmonary oedema/haemoconcentration (classic teaching distinction).
QWhat produces the froth seen at the mouth/nose in drowning?▸

Air, water, mucus and pulmonary surfactant, agitated together by continuing respiratory movements.

Bite marks & Snake bite · Likely tested

Station 4 · Human Bite Mark & Snake Bite First Aid

  • Human bite mark — oval/crescentic pattern of bruising/abrasion; compared against a suspect's dental cast; saliva in the mark yields DNA for identification.
  • Snake bite first aid — immobilise the limb like a splinted fracture, keep at/below heart level, remove constrictive jewellery/clothing, do not cut, suck, apply ice, or a tight tourniquet; transport promptly for antivenom.
QList two things you must never do after a snake bite.▸

Never cut/incise the wound or apply a tight tourniquet — both increase local tissue damage without limiting systemic venom spread.

For2-Tr-028 · Practical reference

Station 5 · Weapon Identification

  • Blunt weapons (lathi, axe handle, stick) — produce contusions, abrasions, and lacerations (irregular margins, tissue bridging).
  • Sharp weapons (knife, dagger, razor, sickle, gandasa) — produce clean incised wounds or stab wounds with sharp margins and no tissue bridging.
  • Firearms — produce characteristic entry/exit wound patterns (see Station 8).
  • Electrical instruments & chemicals — produce Joule burns and corrosive injury respectively, each with their own medico-legal significance.
QHow does a lacerated wound from a blunt weapon differ from a knife's incised wound?▸

Laceration: irregular, ragged margins with tissue bridging (tissue strands crossing the wound). Incised wound: clean, sharp margins with no tissue bridging.

For2-Tr-029/030/031 · Practical reference

Station 6 · Abrasions, Bruise Ageing & Laceration vs Incised Wound

  • Types of abrasion — scratch (linear, nail/thorn), graze (tangential friction, e.g. road-surface skin scraping), pressure abrasion (crushing, patterned to the object), patterned abrasion (reproduces the shape of the causative object, e.g. tyre marks).
  • Bruise ageing by colour change — red/blue initially → green (day 3-5) → yellow (day 5-7) → resolves (~2 weeks), due to sequential haemoglobin breakdown to bilirubin/haemosiderin.
  • Bruise vs postmortem staining (hypostasis) — a true bruise shows tissue infiltration by blood, doesn't shift with position, and can occur anywhere; hypostasis is a fixed, dependent-part discolouration that blanches on pressure early after death and shifts if the body is repositioned before it fixes.
  • Laceration vs incised wound — laceration (blunt force): irregular margins, tissue bridging, bruising around the edges. Incised wound (sharp weapon): clean margins, no tissue bridging, minimal surrounding bruising.
QHow would you age a bruise from its colour alone?▸

Fresh bruises are red/blue; they turn green around day 3-5, yellow around day 5-7, and typically resolve by ~2 weeks — reflecting progressive haemoglobin breakdown.

QWhat is "tissue bridging" and which wound type shows it?▸

Fine strands of connective tissue/vessels crossing the depth of a wound, resisting complete separation — seen in lacerations (blunt trauma), not in incised wounds (sharp weapons).

For2-Tr-032/033/034 · Practical reference

Station 7 · Fractures on X-ray, Qisas & Diyat Act, Injury Certification

  • Fracture types on X-ray — simple (closed, no skin break), compound (open, communicating with skin surface), comminuted (multiple fragments), greenstick (incomplete, bends on one side — typically in children).
  • Qisas & Diyat Act (Pakistan Penal Code) — hurt classification:
    • Itlaf-i-udw — destruction/dismemberment of a limb or organ.
    • Itlaf-i-salahiyyat-i-udw — permanent loss of function of an organ without physical severance.
    • Shajjah — a wound to the head or face, with further subtypes based on depth (up to the bone, fracturing the bone, etc.).
    • Jurh — a wound to any part of the body other than the head/face (termed Jaifah if it penetrates a body cavity).
  • Certification of injury — a medico-legal report documents identification details, history, examination findings, and an opinion on the nature, age, probable weapon, and severity of the injury (whether it amounts to "hurt" under law); it is prepared for and may be requested by the police/court.
QA patient loses function of a hand after a crush injury but the hand is not amputated. Which category applies?▸

Itlaf-i-salahiyyat-i-udw — loss of function of the organ without its physical destruction/severance.

QWhat term applies to a wound on the face that fractures the underlying bone?▸

Shajjah — specifically, a subtype involving bone fracture (as opposed to a wound reaching only skin/soft tissue).

For2-Tr-035 · Practical reference

Station 8 · Firearms & Ballistics

  • Internal ballistics — the mechanics of firing within the weapon (barrel, chamber, trigger, ammunition components: bullet, cartridge case, propellant, primer).
  • External ballistics — factors affecting the bullet's trajectory after leaving the muzzle (gravity, air resistance, wind).
  • Terminal ballistics — the wound produced on impact:
    • Entry wound — typically smaller, with inverted (inward) margins and often an abrasion collar.
    • Exit wound — typically larger, with everted (outward) margins and no abrasion collar.
  • Firing range estimation — presence of tattooing (embedded unburnt powder grains) and scorching/muzzle imprint suggests close/near-contact range; their absence suggests a distant shot.
QHow do you distinguish an entry from an exit gunshot wound?▸

Entry wounds are usually smaller with inverted margins and an abrasion collar; exit wounds are usually larger with everted margins and no abrasion collar.

For2-Tr-036/037 · Practical reference

Station 9 · Burns & Electrocution

  • Burns — classified as flame burn (dry) or scald (wet, from hot liquid/steam); graded 1st (epidermis only) to 4th degree (down to bone); % body surface area estimated using the "rule of nines".
  • Ante-mortem vs postmortem burns — ante-mortem burns show a vital reaction (redness, blistering with fluid, inflammatory response); postmortem burns lack this vital reaction.
  • Electrocution — produces a characteristic "Joule burn" (a firm, blackened, crater-like mark) at the entry point; pattern and severity depend on voltage (low vs high) and the pathway of current through the body (current crossing the heart is most dangerous).
QHow do you tell an ante-mortem burn from a postmortem burn?▸

Ante-mortem burns show a vital reaction — redness, fluid-filled blisters, and an inflammatory response — which is absent in a postmortem burn.

For2-Tr-038/039 · Practical reference

Station 10 · Thermal Extremes, Starvation & Chemical Burns

  • High environmental temperature — heat cramps (mild, muscle cramps with normal temperature), heat exhaustion (weakness, dehydration), heat stroke (life-threatening, core temperature >40°C with CNS dysfunction).
  • Low environmental temperature — frostbite (localised tissue freezing), trench foot / immersion foot (prolonged wet cold exposure without freezing).
  • Starvation — autopsy findings include an empty gastrointestinal tract, loss of subcutaneous fat, and atrophy of internal organs.
  • Chemical burns — caused by corrosive acids/alkalis; autopsy shows corrosion marks tracking along the path of contact/ingestion (lips, mouth, oesophagus, stomach); classified under the Qisas and Diyat Act similarly to thermal burns based on severity/effect.
QWhat autopsy findings suggest death from starvation?▸

An empty GI tract, marked loss of subcutaneous fat, and atrophy of internal organs.

For2-Se-008 · Reference only, not examined

Station 11 · Sexology — Medico-Legal Examination (Practical Reference)

The medico-legal examination of an alleged rape accused/victim requires a structured proforma (identification, history, general and genital examination, injuries noted), with strict chain-of-custody for collection, preservation, labelling and dispatch of specimens (swabs, clothing) to the chemical examiner, following consent and confidentiality protocols.

Medicine

1×OSPE = 08 marks

History-taking for fever, rheumatology examination, and cardiovascular auscultation.

History Taking

Station 1 · Fever (± Headache) — History & Investigations

  • History — onset, duration, pattern (continuous/intermittent/remittent), chills/rigors, headache, rash, joint pains, cough, urinary symptoms, travel/contact/vaccination history.
  • Examination — look for a focus: throat, chest, abdomen, skin, joints.
  • Investigations — CBC, blood culture, urine R/E, malarial parasite (ICT/smear), typhoid serology, CRP/ESR, chest X-ray.
  • Fever + headache specifically raises meningitis/encephalitis on the differential — ask about neck stiffness, photophobia, altered consciousness, and examine for meningeal signs (Kernig's, Brudzinski's) before considering lumbar puncture.
QName three key investigations in a patient with prolonged fever.▸

CBC with differential, blood culture, and malarial parasite/ICT test — supplemented by CRP/ESR and urine R/E.

QWhat two meningeal signs would you check for in fever with headache?▸

Kernig's sign and Brudzinski's sign.

Rheumatology Exam

Station 2 · Hand Examination, RA vs OA & SLE Scenario

  • RA hand exam — symmetric MCP/PIP swelling, ulnar deviation, swan-neck/boutonnière deformities, rheumatoid nodules.
  • Joint swelling history — onset (acute vs chronic), pattern (mono/oligo/polyarticular), symmetry, migratory vs additive, associated systemic features (rash, fever, eye symptoms).
  • SLE scenario (young woman, small-joint arthritis + malar rash) — focused history should probe photosensitivity, oral ulcers, hair loss, frothy urine (renal), chest pain (serositis), Raynaud's, neuropsychiatric symptoms. Investigations: ANA (sensitive), anti-dsDNA/anti-Sm (specific), complement levels (low C3/C4 in active disease).
QWhich antibody is most specific for SLE?▸

Anti-dsDNA (and anti-Sm) — ANA is sensitive but not specific.

QWhat complement pattern is expected in active SLE?▸

Low C3 and C4 — consumed by immune complex formation and activation of the complement cascade.

CVS Exam

Station 3 · Heart Auscultation

  • Areas — mitral (apex), tricuspid (left lower sternal border), pulmonary (2nd left intercostal space), aortic (2nd right intercostal space).
  • Sequence — inspection → palpation → percussion → auscultation.
  • Use the diaphragm for high-pitched sounds, the bell for low-pitched sounds (e.g. the mitral stenosis rumble).
QWhere is the mitral area auscultated?▸

The cardiac apex, typically the 5th left intercostal space, mid-clavicular line.

Medial epicondyle, lateral epicondyle, and olecranon — the triangle relationship is disrupted in posterior elbow dislocation but preserved in a supracondylar fracture.

Pathology & Microbiology

10 scenarios

A 55-year-old smoker presents with persistent cough and weight loss. CXR shows a hilar mass; biopsy shows keratin pearls and intercellular bridges.

a) Diagnosis?▸
Squamous cell carcinoma of the lung.
b) Major risk factor?▸
Cigarette smoking.
c) Associated paraneoplastic syndrome?▸
Hypercalcaemia — tumour secretes PTH-related peptide (PTHrP).

A 10-year-old boy has a limping, waddling gait and cannot climb stairs without using his hands to push off his thighs. Serum CK is markedly raised.

a) Diagnosis?▸
Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
b) Inheritance pattern?▸
X-linked recessive (dystrophin gene mutation).
c) Confirmatory test?▸
Muscle biopsy with dystrophin immunostaining (absent), or genetic testing for dystrophin gene deletion.

A child presents with acute knee pain, fever, and refusal to bear weight. Joint aspirate: turbid fluid, WBC 60,000/µL, predominantly neutrophils.

a) Diagnosis?▸
Septic arthritis.
b) Most likely organism in this age group?▸
Staphylococcus aureus (consider Kingella kingae in children under 5).
c) Immediate management?▸
IV antistaphylococcal antibiotic plus urgent joint aspiration/surgical drainage.

A 45-year-old woman has symmetric pain and swelling of both hands, morning stiffness lasting over an hour, positive RF and anti-CCP.

a) Diagnosis?▸
Rheumatoid arthritis.
b) Expected radiographic finding?▸
Juxta-articular osteopenia, joint space narrowing, marginal erosions — no osteophytes (unlike OA).

A patient has chronic cough, night sweats, and weight loss. CXR shows an upper-lobe cavity; sputum is AFB positive.

a) Diagnosis?▸
Pulmonary tuberculosis.
b) Histology of the granuloma?▸
Caseating granuloma — epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, central caseous necrosis.

A urine culture on CLED agar grows non-lactose-fermenting, oxidase-negative, motile colonies that are H₂S positive on TSI.

a) Likely organism family?▸
Enterobacteriaceae producing H₂S and motile — think Proteus or Salmonella; further biochemical tests (urease, indole) confirm.
b) Why is CLED preferred over blood agar for urine?▸
Its electrolyte-deficient composition inhibits the swarming of Proteus, allowing accurate colony counts.

A sexually active woman presents with vaginal discharge; Gram stain of the discharge shows gram-negative intracellular diplococci.

a) Organism?▸
Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
b) Culture medium of choice?▸
Chocolate agar or Thayer-Martin (selective) medium.

A patient has a painless genital ulcer. VDRL is reactive.

a) Confirmatory test?▸
TPHA/TPPA or FTA-ABS.
b) Stage of syphilis?▸
Primary syphilis (painless chancre).

A child passes an earthworm-like organism (~20 cm) in stool, and has vague abdominal pain.

a) Organism?▸
Ascaris lumbricoides.
b) Complication of a heavy worm load?▸
Intestinal obstruction (or biliary/pancreatic duct obstruction if worms migrate).

A patient with recurrent cyclical fevers has ring forms seen inside RBCs on a peripheral smear.

a) Genus?▸
Plasmodium.
b) Type of anaemia produced?▸
Haemolytic anaemia (RBC destruction by the parasite).

Needle-shaped, negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals.
